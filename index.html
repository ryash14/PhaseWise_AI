Background

Merrowdale Power Networks owns the local electricity network across a region of towns, suburbs and farmland: the substations, the cables in the street, and the service cable and cut-out at every house.

Households are electrifying. Before any of these five technologies is connected to a house, it has to be applied for:

EV charge point

Charges a car

Heat pump

Heats the home

Vehicle-to-grid

Charges a car and can send power back

Solar PV

Generates power

Battery storage

Stores and releases power

The installer applies through one of three portals, Wattcroft, InstallerLine or SiteWren. Each application has a form with the technical answers and statements the installer attached, such as a design statement or load assessment. Every application joins one central queue, and nothing is decided until a Connections Engineer decides it.

Each technology has its own connection standard, a PDF listing the exact requirements it must meet.

The problem

The connections team has three problems

1. Every portal writes it differently

References in three notations. Ratings in amps, kW, W or kVA. Phases in words. Power factor as a percentage. A rating not converted to amps per phase is checked against the wrong limit.

2. Standards that look alike

All five standards share one template. The EV charge point and heat pump standards word their current limit the same way but set different figures. Read the wrong one and the wrong applications pass or fail.

3. Statements contradict the form

A design statement says the charger draws 40 A; the form says 32 A. An engineer working through the queue can miss it.

Customer service has its own limits

  • Advisors may tell a household the technology, the status, and once approved the export limit or load limiting setting.
  • They may never predict whether network work is needed or how long a connection will take.
  • Nobody may give a figure about a substation that fewer than five premises make up, because it would point at one family's home.
→
What Merrowdale needs: screen every application against its own standard, work out whether the network must be assessed first, read every statement sentence, estimate network work and working days, prepare a review with a recommendation, and stop. Nothing is connected, written or sent on the word of a model.

The three phases

Each phase hands its output to the next.

3

Phase 1 · Prepare run once

Input5 messy data extracts (past applications, past connection times, labelled statement sentences) and 16 PDFs (5 standards, Connection Rules, Policy, 8 guides, a casebook)
What happens
  1. Database setup: 6 tables, 14 accounts.
  2. Data pipeline: fix references, convert every rating to amps per phase and kW, phases to 1 or 3, percentages to decimals; remove duplicates and old revisions; a report counts every fix.
  3. Model training: 3 models, each must clear its minimum score.
  4. Knowledge indexing: 16 PDFs into ChromaDB; each standard read back to prove it.
Output → Phase 2Clean data, 3 trained models, searchable rulebooks, accounts

Phase 2 · Check and review

InputThe live queue (40 applications, 102 statements) and everything from Phase 1
Automatic checks (no AI)
  1. Read the form in one set of units: 7.4 kW on one phase ≈ 32 A per phase.
  2. Screen every requirement against its own standard: Met, Not met or Not documented.
  3. Check 3 network triggers: looped service, supply capacity, generation. A blank answer never slips past.
  4. Read every statement sentence; flag ratings that disagree with the form.
  5. Estimate network work likelihood and working days; rank the queue (heating failed first).
CoordinatorOpens an application, checks the results, types what to focus on and opens a review.
AI review5 agents: Planner → Requirements → Evidence → Risk → Writer. Each explains facts the tools established; none calculates.
Output → Phase 3A review with one recommended decision, saved as awaiting decision. The run pauses on disk.

Architecture, explained

SectionWhat it does
A · Phase 1Four scripts, run once in order. Each reads only what the previous one produced.
B · Users and accessThree roles sign in on the Streamlit dashboard. Every request goes to the FastAPI service with a token, which checks the role itself (403 if wrong). The dashboard holds no rules.
C · Review workflowThe application is screened by code, then five agents write the review. The graph pauses at the human decision, saved to checkpoint.db. After the decision it branches: Refer → brief by code; Return → rework request; Approve → approval then household notice. Everything is recorded.
D · Shared servicesScreening and evidence tools (all checks), the 3 models, the knowledge base, Gemini, the two databases, and the chat that sits beside the workflow.

Rules we stick to

  • No requirement in code. Every figure is read from its own standard.
  • Convert before comparing. Amps per phase and kW, exact decimals.
  • Not documented isn't a fail, and a blank never slips past the network.
  • Statements are evidence, never a replacement for the form.
  • Models estimate, code decides, AI only writes.
  • A different engineer decides, and the pause survives a restart.
  • No predictions to customer service; no figures under five premises.
  • Everything is logged, including tokens and time for every AI call.
